# Content Strategy

Content is the primary organic acquisition channel for a vertical agency. The content strategy is designed around one goal: when a dental office manager, immigration paralegal, or MedSpa director starts researching how to fix the operational problem that Veta solves, Veta's content is what they find, and it's the most useful thing on the page.

This strategy does not produce brand awareness content, thought leadership without a specific audience in mind, or content designed to demonstrate that "AI is changing everything." It produces practitioner-level resources that earn trust by being more useful than anything else available for that specific problem.

---

## Content principles

**Write for one person.** Before writing anything, name the specific person: "I'm writing this for the insurance coordinator at a 3-dentist implant practice who is spending 12 hours/week on pre-auth and is starting to wonder if there's a better way." Content written for "dental practices" is too broad. Content written for that insurance coordinator is useful.

**Demonstrate the knowledge before the pitch.** The best pieces have zero sales language. They describe the workflow problem, explain how it works, quantify the cost, describe what good looks like, and let the reader draw the conclusion that they need help. If the content is genuinely useful, the reader already knows by the end that Veta might be worth a conversation.

**Specificity over inspiration.** "You could save hours every week" is not a useful sentence. "A dental insurance coordinator at a 4-provider implant practice typically spends 14–18 hours/week on pre-authorization submissions, follow-ups, and denial tracking — roughly $900–$1,100/week at loaded coordinator rates" is a useful sentence. Readers trust specificity. AI engines cite specificity.

**Publish less, better.** One genuinely useful pillar piece every month beats four thin blog posts every week. Do not trade quality for volume. A piece that earns one backlink from Dental Economics is worth more than 20 pieces that no one links to.

**Update, don't bury.** A published piece that starts ranking should be updated when the information changes, not replaced with a new piece. Update in place, add a "last updated" date, and the SEO equity compounds over time.

## Content types and cadence

### Type 1 — Pillar pieces (monthly, one per cluster)

**What:** 2,500–4,500 words. Definitive, practitioner-level, designed to rank for the cluster's primary query and be cited by AI engines.

**Format:** "The [Role]'s Guide to [Solving Problem]" or "How to [Achieve Outcome] at a [Practice Type]"

**Structure (every pillar piece follows this):**
1. Opening: the specific problem, quantified. Who has it, what it costs them, why the current approach doesn't work. (200–300 words)
2. Why this problem exists: the structural reason — legacy software, manual process dependencies, economics of the workflow. Not blaming the practice. Explaining the system. (300–400 words)
3. What the workflow looks like today (step-by-step): practitioner-level process map of the current manual workflow. This section proves vertical knowledge. (400–600 words)
4. What good looks like: a description of the automated/improved workflow, step by step, with specific tools and agent names. Concrete and specific. (400–600 words)
5. What it takes to get there: technical requirements, typical timeline, what needs to change. Honest about complexity. (300–400 words)
6. Common mistakes and how to avoid them: based on real patterns, not invented scenarios. (200–300 words)
7. FAQs: answer the 5–8 most common questions practitioners have about this cluster. Structured for AI citation. (300–500 words)
8. Closing: one specific next step. Not "contact us." A next step the reader can take right now (download a checklist, read a related piece, or book a discovery call if they've read this far and want to talk).

**Production process:**
- Week 1: Outline and research (confirm benchmarks, identify any gaps in vertical knowledge)
- Week 2: First draft
- Week 3: Review and edit — read it as the target practitioner, not as the writer
- Week 4: Final edits, SEO optimization (title, meta, internal links, schema), publish

**Who writes it:** Founder. These cannot be outsourced without losing the practitioner voice that makes them credible. Once there are 6+ published pillar pieces, an editor or co-author becomes viable.

**Publication destination:** Primary website. Cross-post excerpts to LinkedIn (not the full piece — drive traffic back to the site).

### Type 2 — Problem/solution pieces (2–3 per month)

**What:** 800–1,500 words. More specific than a pillar piece, targets one narrow query or one specific practitioner question.

**Format:** "Why [specific problem happens]", "The real cost of [workflow]", "[Number] things [role] can stop doing manually"

**Production process:** One week, start to finish. These are faster to write because the scope is narrow. Outline on Monday, draft Tuesday–Wednesday, edit and publish Thursday.

**Examples by vertical:**

Dental:
- "Why Your Treatment Coordinator's Follow-Up Calls Aren't Working (And What Does)"
- "The Pre-Authorization Process at Every Major Dental Insurer: A Coordinator's Guide"
- "What Happens to New Patient Inquiries That Come In After Hours?"
- "Dental Morning Huddle: What Your EHR Already Knows That Your Team Doesn't"
- "The Difference Between a Dental Recall Campaign and a Dental Recall System"

Legal:
- "How Much Time Does a Paralegal Spend on I-485 Preparation? (The Real Numbers)"
- "Why Immigration Clients Call to Ask 'What's the Status' — And How to Stop It"
- "Missed USPTO Deadlines: How They Happen at Small IP Firms"
- "The Intake-to-Engagement-Letter Timeline: What's Normal and What's Losing You Clients"
- "What Clio's Workflow Automation Can and Can't Do"

Aesthetic medicine:
- "The Consultation Conversion Gap: Why Most MedSpas Are Leaving 30% of Revenue on the Table"
- "Post-Treatment Follow-Up in Aesthetics: What Patients Actually Want to Hear"
- "Why Your MedSpa Gets Fewer Google Reviews Than You Deserve"
- "The Pre-Treatment Scramble: What Happens When a Patient Arrives Without Their Paperwork"
- "Botox Club Membership: Why Renewals Fall Through the Cracks"

### Type 3 — Integration and reference guides (monthly)

**What:** 600–1,200 words. Technical, specific, reference-oriented. Answers the question "what is it possible to do with [tool]" or "how does [integration] work."

**Who reads these:** Practitioners who are technically curious, practice managers who have been told to "look into automation," and decision-makers validating whether their stack is compatible with what they're considering.

**Examples:**
- "Open Dental API: What's Available, What's Not, and What It Means for Automation"
- "Clio Manage and Workflow Automation: A Practical Guide"
- "Nextech API for MedSpas: Integration Capabilities and Limitations"
- "How Weave's API Works — and When You Need Something More"
- "Dentrix vs. Open Dental for Automation: Which Is Easier to Build On?"
- "What Data Lives in Your EHR That You're Not Using"

**Production process:** Faster than Type 2 because they're factual, not analytical. Confirm technical accuracy with the integration documentation or by building a test connection.

---

### Type 4 — Benchmark and data pages (quarterly)

**What:** A structured reference page of key metrics, benchmarks, and statistics for a vertical or cluster. Not a blog post — a permanent reference page that gets updated quarterly.

**Why:** These become the most-cited pages over time because they're the best single source for the numbers that everyone cites. "According to Veta's benchmark data..." is a citation that gets picked up by both human writers and AI engines.

**Format:** Tables and bullet lists, not prose. Source every number (even if the source is "Veta internal analysis of [N] engagements" — intellectual honesty matters more than appearing to have external sources for everything).

**Examples:**
- "Specialty Dental Operations Benchmarks: Pre-Auth Times, Conversion Rates, Staff Time by Workflow"
- "Legal Practice Efficiency Benchmarks: Hours per Matter by Practice Area and Firm Size"
- "Aesthetic Medicine Conversion Benchmarks: Consultation-to-Booking Rates by Treatment Type"

**Note:** In year one, before real engagement data is available, these pages will be seeded with industry-cited benchmarks (from ADA surveys, AILA research, ASAPS data, etc.) plus conservative estimates labeled as estimates. As engagement data accumulates, replace estimates with real numbers.

---

### Type 5 — Case studies (as engagements are completed)

**What:** 600–1,000 words. Real client result, real numbers, real workflow before and after. The single most persuasive content type — but only publishable after a completed engagement and explicit client permission.

**Format:**
- Practice type, size, location (can be anonymized if client prefers)
- The problem: which cluster, what the current workflow looked like, what it cost
- What was built: which agents, which integrations, how long it took
- The result: specific numbers at 30 and 90 days (staff hours recovered, conversion rate change, revenue recovered, etc.)
- One quote from the client (or anonymized paraphrase if they prefer)

**When to pursue permission:** At the handoff milestone, as part of the retainer offer conversation. Frame it as: "We'd like to publish an anonymized case study about this engagement. We'd describe the practice type and size, the workflow we automated, and the measured results — with your review before we publish anything. Would you be open to that?"

Do not publish case studies without written client approval. Do not exaggerate results. The case study should be the most conservative accurate description of what happened.

## Distribution

**Primary distribution channels:**

| Channel | Content types | Frequency | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agency website (blog/resources) | All types | Per production schedule | SEO, GEO, primary destination |
| LinkedIn (founder personal) | Excerpts from Type 1 + Type 2 + case studies | 2–3x/week when content is live | Reach practitioners in network, signal vertical expertise |
| Vertical email list (build over time) | Type 1 + Type 4 + case studies | Monthly digest | Direct to practitioners who've opted in |
| Partner distribution (via referral partners) | Type 1 + Type 4 | When partners distribute to their lists | Borrowed audience in the exact vertical |
| Practitioner community posts (Dental Town, ABA forums, etc.) | Tier B pieces that directly answer forum questions | As relevant questions arise | Inbound links, community credibility |

**What not to do:**
- Post every piece on every channel immediately — let it breathe on the website first for indexing
- Post the full article on LinkedIn — excerpt the most useful 200 words and link back
- Chase social media metrics — follower counts and likes are not the goal; qualified inbound inquiries are

**The minimum viable month:** One published piece that is genuinely better than what exists for its target query. A month with zero published content compounds into a SEO gap that takes months to recover. A month with one excellent piece compounds into authority.

## Voice and style

**Voice:** Practitioner to practitioner. Not "AI company marketing to dental practices" — that reads wrong. The voice is "someone who knows your workflow, has seen what breaks, and is telling you directly what they've observed."

**Style rules:**
- No jargon that practitioners don't use themselves (don't say "leverage AI capabilities" — say "automate the pre-auth submission")
- Numbers over adjectives ("saves 14 hours/week" not "saves significant time")
- Active voice over passive ("the agent submits the pre-auth" not "pre-auths are submitted by the agent")
- Short paragraphs — most readers are skimming on a device, not reading a manuscript
- No em-dash abuse — one per paragraph maximum
- No filler openers — never start a piece with "In today's..." or "As technology evolves..." or "Many dental practices are..."

**Test:** Read the first paragraph out loud. Would a dental office manager read this and think "this person understands my job?" If yes, continue. If no, rewrite before publishing.
