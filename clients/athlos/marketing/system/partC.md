# 3 · How To Film — The Production Playbook

This is the most hands-on chapter in the manual, and the most important one for the founder-as-face model. Everything upstream — positioning, the funnel, the offer — is worthless if we can't reliably put Ian on camera and produce content the team can turn into leads.

Read this as a build spec. By the end you should be able to walk into a session, shoot 5–10 pieces, and hand a clean folder of footage to editing without anyone improvising.

**The one rule that governs this whole chapter:** *Ian is the only one who can be on camera. Everyone else builds the machine around him.* We design every part of production so that Ian's job is small, repeatable, and low-friction — show up, be a person, talk — and the team absorbs all the complexity (kit, setup, shot lists, files, editing, packaging). If a decision makes Ian's on-camera job harder, it's the wrong decision. See §3.7.

**Iman lens:** Iman spends only 3–5 hours/month actually creating; his team does the rest ("effortless front, best-in-class backend"). We can't match that team yet — but we build toward it from day one by making Ian's part tiny and the pipeline around him as systematized as our size allows.

**Cold-start reality:** we are one founder also shipping the app, a tiny warm list, organic, ~30 days. We do NOT have a studio, a videographer, or a clipper army. This chapter is deliberately phone-first and batching-first, because a solo founder's scarcest resource is on-camera time. **Batching is the answer to that scarcity — it is the load-bearing idea of this chapter (§3.5).**

## 3.1 · The Kit — Phone-First Gear List

We shoot on a phone. Not "for now until we can afford a camera" — on purpose. A modern phone shoots better video than most people's cameras, it's always in Ian's pocket, and it removes every excuse to not film. Iman's own rule: *good audio + a clean background beats fancy gear.* Fancy gear is a way to procrastinate. We reject it.

The single biggest quality upgrade is not the camera — it's the **microphone**. Viewers forgive mediocre video; they will not forgive bad audio. They click away in the first two seconds if it sounds echoey or hollow. So the mic is the one place we spend money first (§3.2 explains why).

### The list (cheap vs better)

| Item | Cheap / start-today | Better (when there's budget) | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Camera** | The phone Ian already owns (iPhone with 4K is ideal; any recent phone is fine) | Same phone — don't upgrade the camera before the mic | It's already good enough. Reject the camera-upgrade excuse. |
| **Microphone** | Wired lavalier (clip-on) into the phone (~€20–30), OR the phone's own mic in a small quiet room as a last resort | Wireless lav kit (DJI Mic Mini / Rode Wireless-type, ~€100–150) — clips on, no cable, moves with Ian | **The #1 upgrade.** Audio > video, always. A €25 lav beats a €1,000 camera with the built-in mic. |
| **Tripod / stability** | Cheap phone tripod + phone clamp (~€15), or lean the phone against books | Small tripod with a phone mount + a gimbal (DJI Osmo-type, ~€100) for walking/movement B-roll | Static talking-head = tripod. Movement/B-roll = gimbal. Shaky footage reads as amateur. |
| **Lighting** | A window (free — see §3.2). North-facing, soft daylight. | One softbox or a large ring/soft LED panel (~€40–80) for consistent indoor light regardless of weather | Consistent light = a recognizable, professional look across the batch. Window light is genuinely great — but it changes with the weather, which breaks batching consistency. |
| **Storage / files** | Phone's own storage, cleared before a session; back up to cloud (Google Drive / iCloud) after | A fast USB-C SSD or a paid cloud tier for 4K footage | 4K footage is huge. Running out of space mid-session kills a Content Day. Always start a session with space cleared. |

### "You can start today with just X"

**You can start today with: your phone, a €25 clip-on lav mic, a €15 tripod, and a window.** That's the entire minimum viable kit. Total under €50. Everything else on the list is an optimization we add later, not a prerequisite. If Ian is waiting on gear to start, he's procrastinating — the excuse is already dead.

## 3.2 · The Setup — How To Physically Shoot

This is the checklist the team sets up *before* Ian sits down. Ian should walk into a room where framing, background, light, and audio are already handled. His job is to talk; the setup is ours.

### Framing

- **Headroom:** leave a small, consistent gap above Ian's head — not too much (floating in space), not cropping the top of his head. A thumb's width at the top of frame.
- **Rule of thirds:** Ian's eyes sit on the **upper third** line, not dead-center. Turn on the phone's grid (Settings → Camera → Grid on iPhone) and place the eyes on that top horizontal line every time. This is the single fastest way to make a shot look intentional instead of like a webcam.
- **Eye-line:** Ian looks slightly into the lens, not down at it. The phone should be at roughly **eye height or a touch above** — never below (shooting up the nose is unflattering and reads amateur). Prop the tripod up to eye level.
- **Distance:** medium-close. Chest-up for talking-head. Close enough to feel personal and intimate, not a security-camera wide shot. For vertical (Reels/Shorts), frame a little tighter — head and shoulders — because phones are held close.

### Background — what reads well

We have three environments, each with a job:

- **Gym / training floor** *(the hero environment).* This is where ATHLOS content should live most of the time — it's on-brand, it's proof, it signals "athlete, not influencer at a desk." Racks, turf, a court in the background. Depth behind Ian (don't back him flat against a wall — a few meters of gym behind him gives professional depth-of-field). This is the default for pillars 2 (science with Tim) and 3 (Ian training).
- **Home / clean indoor** *(THE JOURNEY / build-in-public).* A tidy, simple background — a plain wall with one or two intentional objects (a plant, a ball, nothing cluttered). This is where the founder-story, "dan X/30," and build-in-public talking-heads live. Clean beats fancy. Kill visual clutter — a messy background is the fastest way to look unprofessional.
- **Outdoors** *(reach / atmosphere).* A track, a pitch, a court, a stadium exterior. Great for hooks and B-roll, high energy, but **only shoot outdoors when the weather cooperates** — wind ruins audio (a lav windscreen/foam cover helps) and changing light breaks consistency. Treat outdoors as a bonus environment, not the reliable one.

**Rule:** pick a background that says "athlete" and has depth, and keep it consistent within a batch so the pieces feel like a series.

### Lighting

- **Natural window light is the free hero.** Ian faces the window (light on his face), never with the window behind him (that silhouettes him — the phone exposes for the bright window and Ian goes dark). Soft, indirect daylight from a north-facing window is flattering and free.
- **Ring light or soft LED panel** for consistency when daylight isn't available or when batching across hours (the sun moves; a lamp doesn't). Position it in front of Ian, slightly above eye level, angled down — mimicking natural overhead light.
- **Avoid:** harsh overhead ceiling lights alone (they cast raccoon shadows under the eyes); mixed color temperatures (a warm lamp + cool daylight makes skin look sickly — pick one); backlighting that silhouettes; and any flickering light (cheap LEDs can strobe on camera).

### Audio — why audio beats video

If we get one thing right, it's this. **Bad audio makes people leave in the first two seconds; mediocre video does not.** The brain tolerates a soft image; it will not tolerate echo, hiss, or hollow room sound. Concretely:

- **Use the lav mic, always.** Clip it on Ian's shirt, roughly a hand's width below the chin, centered. Hide the cable if wired. Test-record 5 seconds and play it back before the real take — every session.
- **Shoot in a quiet, soft room.** Hard empty rooms echo. Rooms with soft furnishings (couch, curtains, rugs, clothes) sound better. Turn off fans, AC, the fridge hum, notifications. A gym can be echoey and loud — for talking-head *in* a gym, still rely on the lav close to the mouth, and shoot when the gym is quiet.
- **Never rely on the phone's built-in mic for talking-head** unless you're in a genuinely small, soft, silent room and have no lav. The lav is the whole difference between "amateur" and "clean."

### Phone settings (set these once, lock them per session)

- **Resolution / fps:** 4K at 30fps for the standard look. 4K gives editing room to crop and reframe (important for cutting one horizontal long-form into vertical shorts — §3.6). Use 60fps only when you specifically want slow-motion B-roll (training clips).
- **Lock exposure and focus:** on iPhone, tap-and-hold on Ian's face until "AE/AF LOCK" appears. This stops the shot from hunting — auto-exposure drifting brighter/darker mid-sentence, or focus breathing in and out, both scream amateur. Lock it on the face before every take.
- **Orientation — per platform:**
  - **Vertical (9:16)** for Reels, Shorts, TikTok — this is where most of our reach lives, so it's the default for short-form.
  - **Horizontal (16:9)** for the YouTube long-form / VSL — the core converting video (per `04-funnel-sales-system.md §5.1`) is shot horizontal.
  - **The pro move:** shoot the long-form horizontal in 4K, framed with enough room around Ian that editing can *crop it to vertical* for shorts. One shoot, both formats (§3.6).

## 3.3 · On-Camera Delivery — How Ian Should Present

This is the part only Ian can do, so it deserves the most care. The goal is not "be a polished presenter." The goal is **be a real, high-conviction person the viewer trusts.** In a small Slovenian market where authenticity is the whole moat (per `01-brand-foundation.md §3` — Ian is the founder-athlete, the person IS the moat), polish that reads as "influencer" actually *hurts* us. We want one-of-one, not one-of-many.

**Iman lens:** packaging and the first seconds are 80% of the game; and the founder's job is to be *known and trusted*, not slick. Ian doesn't need to perform — he needs to be *the guy building this, on camera.*

### Energy

- Bring energy **one notch above** how you feel in a normal conversation. The camera flattens energy — what feels like "a bit much" in the room reads as "normal and engaged" on screen. Flat, low-energy delivery is the #1 reason good content dies.
- Not fake-hyped, not a caffeine ad — just *lean in, mean it.* Ian genuinely cares about this (he's building it and training on it); let that conviction show.

### The first-3-seconds hook rule

The first three seconds decide whether anyone watches the rest. No slow intros, no "hey guys, welcome back," no logo animation. Open on the most interesting sentence. State the hook, on camera, immediately — or lead with a visual that stops the scroll.

Slovenian hook patterns Ian can open cold with (idiomatic, spoken, not written):

- *"Če treniraš nogomet in delaš klasičen 'split' iz Instagrama — delaš se počasnejšega. Razložim."*
- *"Nihče v Sloveniji ti ne pove tega o hitrosti. Zato sem zgradil aplikacijo, ki ti."*
- *"Dan 12 od 30. Danes se je skoraj vse sesulo."*
- *"To je največja napaka, ki jo dela vsak mlad košarkaš pri treningu eksplozivnosti."*
- *"Osebni trener + kineziolog + fizioterapevt = 400 evrov na mesec. Ali pa to."*
- *"Ne verjameš, da lahko AI naredi pravi trening za tvoj šport? Poglej."*

The pattern: name the enemy, or promise a specific payoff, or open mid-story — in the first sentence, before anything else.

### Pacing

- Talk a little faster than feels natural and **cut every dead moment in editing** (§3.6). Short-form has no room for "umm," pauses, or throat-clearing.
- One idea per breath. Deliver a point, breathe, next point. This gives editing clean cut points.
- Vary the rhythm — a fast run of sentences, then one slow, weighted line for emphasis. Monotone pacing loses people even if the content is good.

### Eye contact with the lens

- Look at **the lens itself**, not at your own face on the screen. Put a tiny sticker or arrow next to the lens as a reminder. Looking at the screen means looking slightly off — the viewer feels talked-past instead of talked-to.
- Direct lens eye contact is what creates the parasocial "he's talking to me" feeling that builds trust and, downstream, leads.

### Talk like a person, not a presenter

- Speak the way Ian would explain this to a training partner in the gym, not the way he'd narrate a corporate video. Contractions, real Slovenian, the odd bit of slang the athlete audience uses.
- **Read vs speak:** do NOT read a script word-for-word off the screen — it always looks read (the eyes track, the delivery flattens). Instead: know the 3 beats you want to hit (hook → point → CTA), then *talk* through them naturally. Bullet points on a note, not a paragraph to recite. The one exception is the tightly-scripted hook line — that's worth nailing verbatim.
- If a take comes out stiff, say the line out loud once as if venting to a friend, then roll — the second, looser version is almost always the keeper.

### Hands and gestures

- Use hands naturally — gesturing on camera reads as confidence and energy, and it's how Ian talks anyway. Frozen, hands-at-sides delivery looks nervous.
- Keep gestures within the frame (don't wave out of shot). For chest-up framing, gestures near the chest/face read best.

### Retakes

- **Retakes are free and expected.** Fluff a line? Don't stop, don't sigh, don't restart the whole thing — pause for one beat and say the line again. Editing keeps the good take and cuts the flub. This keeps energy up and avoids the death spiral of "let me start over" (attempt 8 is always worse than attempt 2).
- Cap it: if a piece won't come out right after ~3 tries, move on and come back. Grinding one clip kills the whole Content Day's momentum.

### Do / Don't

**DO:**
- Bring energy one notch up.
- Hook in the first 3 seconds.
- Look at the lens.
- Talk like you're in the gym, not on stage.
- Use your hands.
- Retake the line, not the whole clip.
- Be specific and opinionated — take a side (against generic splits, against aesthetics-only training).

**DON'T:**
- Don't open slow ("hey guys, so today…").
- Don't read a script off the screen.
- Don't go monotone or low-energy.
- Don't look at your own image instead of the lens.
- Don't try to be a polished influencer — be the real founder-athlete.
- Don't restart the whole take over one flubbed word.
- Don't be vague or "balanced" — vague content converts nobody.

## 3.4 · B-Roll & Visuals — Show, Don't Just Tell

Talking-head alone is fine, but the pieces that travel *show* something. B-roll (supplementary footage cut over the talking) does three jobs: it holds attention (visual change every few seconds), it hides edit cuts, and — most importantly for us — it delivers **product proof**. "Show, don't just tell" is not a style choice here; it's how we prove a cold audience that the app is real.

### What to grab (a standing shot list to collect whenever possible)

- **Training clips of Ian:** sprints, plyometrics, court/pitch work, lifts. Shoot these in 4K/60fps so editing can slow-mo the explosive moments. This is proof for pillar 3 ("treniram kot športnik") and endlessly reusable B-roll.
- **App screen-recordings (the highest-value B-roll we have):** record the phone screen showing the ATHLOS app *working* — the Coach generating a real plan, the daily report, biometrija, the season calendar. This is the product-proof that turns "cool idea" into "I want that." Capture clean screen-recordings deliberately, not as an afterthought.
- **Tim / science footage:** Tim explaining or demonstrating a protocol, whiteboard/diagram moments, anything that visually signals real expertise (pillar 2).
- **Sport footage / atmosphere:** the pitch, the court, the gym filling up, a ball, cleats, the environment. Establishes the world and covers cuts.
- **Hands / detail shots:** lacing boots, chalking up, tapping through the app on a phone. Detail shots make an edit feel produced.

### The product-proof rule

Every time we make a claim about the app ("it knows your sport," "it periodizes around your season," "it adapts"), we should have footage of the app **doing exactly that** to cut over the claim. Capturing product-proof screen-recordings is a standing task, not a one-off — build a small library of them so any editor can pull the right clip. A claim over a shot of the feature working is worth ten claims over a talking head.

## 3.5 · The Content Day / Batching SOP

This is the operational heart of the founder-as-face model. Ian's scarcest resource is on-camera time — he's also building the app. So we do **not** film daily. We batch: **one Content Day per week, produce 5–10 pieces in one session.** Set up once, dress once, get into camera-energy once, and walk out with a week-plus of content. This is the direct answer to the solo-founder time blind spot.

**Iman lens:** the Content Day is Iman's explicit beginner system — batch-film in one focused block, let the team handle the rest. We run it as a literal repeatable SOP so it survives Ian being busy.

### Step-by-step SOP

**PHASE A — Prep (team does this BEFORE the day; Ian just approves)**

1. **Build the shot list.** The team assembles a numbered list of the 5–10 pieces to film: each with its hook line (Slovenian, written out), the 3 beats, the pillar it serves (JOURNEY / science / training), the CTA, and the intended format (vertical short vs horizontal long). Ian reviews it the day before — his only prep job.
2. **Group by setup.** Reorder the shot list so all pieces sharing a background/outfit/location are filmed back-to-back. Filming order follows *setup*, not topic — never tear down and rebuild a setup mid-day.
3. **Plan outfits.** Ian brings 2–3 different tops (and swaps mid-day) so batch-filmed pieces don't all look identical when posted across a week — otherwise viewers notice it was all shot in one session. Keep each outfit consistent within its block.
4. **Confirm locations & timing.** Book the gym/court for a quiet window. If outdoors is on the list, check the weather and slot it when light is good.
5. **Charge and clear.** Phone charged, storage cleared (enough free space for 4K), mic batteries charged, backup power ready.

**PHASE B — Setup (team, ~15 min at the start of the session)**

6. **Rig the shot.** Tripod at eye height, framing per §3.2 (grid, thirds, headroom), background set with depth, light in front of Ian, mic clipped on.
7. **Lock settings.** 4K/30 (or /60 for training), grid on, exposure + focus locked on Ian's face.
8. **Audio + framing test.** Record 5 seconds, play it back — check the audio is clean and the framing is right. Do NOT skip this. Discovering bad audio *after* a 3-hour session is a catastrophe.

**PHASE C — Filming (Ian on, team runs it)**

9. **Warm up.** First one or two clips are throwaways to get Ian into camera-energy. Don't publish clip #1.
10. **Film in setup-blocks.** Work through the shot list in order. For each piece: team reads the number and hook, Ian delivers, team confirms it's usable (or calls a retake), then moves to the next. Ian never manages the list — the team does; Ian just performs.
11. **Grab the B-roll (§3.4)** while everything is already set up — training clips, app screen-recordings, detail shots. Never leave a session without B-roll.
12. **Slate lightly.** Team notes which take is the keeper for each numbered piece (a quick note, or Ian claps/says the number) so editing isn't hunting through 40 clips.

**PHASE D — File handling & handoff (team, immediately after)**

13. **Back up first, immediately.** Offload all footage from the phone to two places (cloud + a drive) *before* clearing the phone. Never delete originals until the backup is verified. Losing a Content Day to a full phone or a lost file is unacceptable.
14. **Organize into a folder** named by date, e.g. `2026-07-11-content-day/`, with subfolders `talking-head/`, `b-roll/`, `app-screens/`. Match clips to the shot-list numbers.
15. **Hand off to editing** with the shot list attached — each piece's number, hook, keeper-take note, format, caption direction, and CTA. Editing should never have to guess intent.
16. **Log it** on the content calendar: what was shot, what it becomes, when each piece publishes across the coming week(s).

**The output of one Content Day:** 5–10 talking-head pieces + a batch of B-roll + app-proof clips → enough for a week-plus of posting, all handed to editing in a clean folder. That is the entire founder-time cost of a week of content: one session.

## 3.6 · Editing & Packaging

Editing turns raw footage into scroll-stopping pieces; packaging decides whether anyone sees them at all. Per the Iman lens, **packaging is ~80% of the outcome** — so we treat the thumbnail and title as more important than the edit itself.

### The editing style (short-form, Slovenian)

- **Fast pace, no dead air.** Cut every pause, "umm," and breath. Short-form should feel a touch faster than comfortable. If a moment isn't earning its place, cut it.
- **Captions / subtitles are MANDATORY** for Slovenian short-form. Most people watch on mute; without burned-in Slovenian captions, the piece is dead on arrival. Auto-generate then **hand-correct** — auto-captions butcher Slovenian, and wrong subtitles look careless. Style them big, bold, high-contrast, one or two words highlighted as spoken.
- **Hook on screen in the first second.** Put the hook as text on screen immediately, backing up the spoken hook — it stops the scroll for muted viewers.
- **B-roll cutaways every few seconds** (§3.4) — cut to training, app screens, detail shots over the talking. Visual change holds attention and hides edit cuts.
- **Keep it tight.** For short-form, shorter usually wins — cut to the shortest version that still lands the point and the CTA.

### The tools

- **CapCut** for short-form — fast, mobile or desktop, good auto-captions (still hand-correct the Slovenian), easy for the team to run without a pro editor. This is the default.
- **Premiere / DaVinci Resolve** for the long-form / VSL — the core converting video deserves a proper edit. Optional; CapCut can carry the whole operation at cold-start scale.

### Packaging = 80%

For anything with a thumbnail (YouTube long-form, some Reels covers), the thumbnail and title do most of the work. Two disciplines:

**Thumbnail — the Rule of Three** (Iman: thumbnail is engineering, not art — optimize for click-through, ignore "pretty"). A strong thumbnail has exactly three elements:
1. **Ian's face with a genuine, readable emotion** (intense, surprised, determined).
2. **Big, readable text** — 3–5 words max, legible on a phone screen.
3. **One context visual** — the app on screen, a training moment, a "vs" comparison.

**Title discipline:** decide the title + thumbnail *before* filming (Iman's hard-won lesson — package first, shoot second), so the piece is built to deliver on the packaging. Specific and curiosity-driven, never generic.

**Three example SL title + thumbnail concepts:**

1. **Title:** *"Zakaj te 'klasičen split' dela POČASNEJŠEGA (nogomet)"*
   **Thumbnail:** Ian mid-sprint, intense face · text "POČASNEJŠI?" · small inset of a crossed-out Instagram workout split.
2. **Title:** *"Zgradil sem AI trenerja za slovenske športnike. Poglej, kako dela."*
   **Thumbnail:** Ian holding the phone toward camera, app plan visible on screen · text "AI TRENER" · gym behind him.
3. **Title:** *"30 dni treniram po svoji aplikaciji. Teden 1."*
   **Thumbnail:** Ian mid-plyo jump, determined · text "DAN 1 → 30" · the ATHLOS report visible as the context visual.

### One long-form → many shorts

The long-form (or VSL) is the mother asset. Because it was shot horizontal in 4K with room around Ian (§3.2), editing crops it to vertical and pulls out the self-contained moments — a strong hook, a punchy science point, a proof moment — as standalone shorts.

**Cold-start honesty:** per `00-START-HERE.md`, we do NOT run the full 10-shorts-from-one-video remix engine yet — nobody's watching enough for the math to matter, and it's editing time we don't have. We cut the **genuinely good** moments into a handful of shorts and ignore the quota. The remix engine is a month-2+ scaling lever once there's an audience and a back-catalog (Iman's "Content Rehashing" — noted, not used at launch).

## 3.7 · The Founder-as-Face Guardrail

This is the line that makes the whole system work. Draw it explicitly so nobody blurs it.

### ONLY Ian can do:

- **Be on camera** — his face, his presence, his energy. This is un-delegable. It's the entire trust engine (`01-brand-foundation.md §3`: the person IS the moat).
- **Be the voice** — the opinions, the takes, the founder story, THE JOURNEY. The point of view is Ian's; it can't be ghost-performed.
- **Show up to the Content Day and perform** — deliver the hooks, do the retakes, grab the training B-roll.
- **Approve the shot list** the day before (his one prep task) and, ultimately, own that the content sounds like *him*.

That's it. That is Ian's entire job in this pipeline.

### The team does EVERYTHING else:

- Builds and researches the shot list and hooks (§3.5 Phase A).
- Owns all the kit, setup, framing, lighting, audio rig, phone settings (§3.1–3.2).
- Runs the Content Day — reads hooks, calls retakes, manages the list, tracks keeper-takes (§3.5 Phase C).
- Handles all files: backup, organization, handoff (§3.5 Phase D).
- Does all editing, captioning, B-roll cutaways (§3.6).
- Does all packaging — thumbnails, titles, the Rule of Three (§3.6).
- Schedules, posts, and tracks everything on the content calendar.

**The design principle:** every task that *can* be lifted off Ian, *is* lifted off Ian — so his scarce on-camera time is the only thing he spends on marketing. The more the team absorbs, the more content Ian can be the face of without it eating the time he needs to ship the app.

### Blind-spot check — the solo-founder time trap

The honest risk: at cold-start we may not have a full team yet, and some of "the team does everything else" lands back on Ian by default. **Batching is the mitigation.** By compressing all of Ian's on-camera time into one weekly Content Day, we cap his marketing time-cost to a single predictable block — even if he's temporarily also doing some editing. As the team fills in (per `09-team-marketing-protocol.md`), each pipeline task moves off Ian in priority order: first the file-handling and scheduling, then editing/captions, then packaging, then shot-list research — until Ian's only job really is *show up on Content Day and be the face.* That is the target state, and this chapter is the spec for reaching it.

**One-sentence version:** *Ian films one focused Content Day a week and is the face and the voice; the team builds, shoots-around, edits, packages, and posts everything else — and batching is what keeps the founder's time-cost to that single weekly block.*
